Why I went back to do a PhD after almost a decade in industry

September 2026 · 5 min read

By 2020 I had been working in software for almost a decade. I'd started in test automation in the Netherlands, moved to the UK, and was building banking and fraud systems as a consultant. I was good at my job and I enjoyed it.

Then I decided to go back to university and do a PhD in Artificial Intelligence. A few people thought I was taking a step backwards. Looking back, it was one of the best decisions I've made.

Why I did it

Working on fraud detection, I kept running into the same feeling. I could build the systems around a model, but I didn't fully understand why one approach worked and another didn't. I was using AI without really understanding it, and that bothered me.

I could see AI was going to change the kind of software I'd spent my career building. I wanted to understand it properly, not just know how to call it.

It wasn't an ivory tower

My PhD was funded through an EPSRC industrial award in partnership with Arup, so it was never purely academic. I worked on using machine learning and optimisation to plan electric vehicle charging infrastructure, with real transport and energy data that was messy, incomplete and sometimes contradictory.

The models we built reduced operational costs by 22% and improved infrastructure utilisation by 35%. I also published work in journals like iScience and IEEE Access. But the part I remember most is how much time we spent arguing about the data, not the algorithms. That turned out to be excellent preparation for working on AI in banks.

What research taught me about engineering

Research forces you to be honest. A reviewer doesn't care how hard you worked; they care whether your results hold up. You learn to ask "how do we know this is true?" and to be suspicious of results that look too good.

I bring that habit into every AI project I lead. When a model looks amazing on a few examples, I want to see how it performs on the cases nobody picked by hand. When someone says an agent is "basically ready", I ask what we're comparing it against. It isn't about being negative. It's about not fooling ourselves.

The most useful thing a PhD taught me wasn't a technique. It was the habit of asking how we'd know if we were wrong.

What research taught me about leading people

During my PhD I led three applied research teams, with software engineers, data scientists and people from industry. Nobody reported to me formally. I couldn't tell anyone what to do; I had to convince them it was worth doing.

That was the best management training I've had. It taught me that people do their best work when they understand the "why" and have real ownership of the "how". When I later went on to lead 26 engineers, I relied on exactly the same lesson.

Would I recommend it?

Not to everyone. A PhD is long, it pays badly, and there are days when you wonder why you left a comfortable job. But if you're an experienced engineer who keeps hitting the edge of what you understand, going deep for a few years can change the rest of your career.

For me, it closed the gap between building software and understanding AI. Today my job is to lead teams that do both, and I don't think I could do it as well without having done each of them myself.
